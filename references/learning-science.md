# Learning Science & Techniques

Reference for the techniques used in the musical-mnemonic approach.  
Includes both well-supported learning principles and experimental or plausible mechanisms.  
The goal is transparency: what was tried, why, and what the evidence looks like.

## Established Techniques

### Dual Coding
Presenting the same information in both auditory (song) and visual (subtitles or simple images) form.  
**Why included:** Reduces cognitive load and improves retention by giving two retrieval routes.  
**Reference:** Mayer’s multimedia learning principles; Paivio’s dual-coding theory.

### Spaced Repetition / Massed-then-Spaced Listening
Heavy initial repetition (massed) followed by increasing gaps between reviews.  
**Why included:** One of the most robust findings in learning science for long-term retention.  
**Reference:** Ebbinghaus; Cepeda et al. on distributed practice; Bjork’s desirable difficulties.

### Active Recall / Retrieval Practice
Singing, speaking, or producing the target material rather than only passively listening.  
**Why included:** Retrieval strengthens memory more than re-exposure alone.  
**Reference:** Roediger & Karpicke; the testing effect literature.

### Moderate Tempo & Rhythmic Entrainment (~90 BPM)
Steady, moderate tempo that supports both attention and a light entrainment effect without becoming frantic.  
**Why included:** Anecdotal and some music-cognition work suggests moderate tempos aid sustained attention and absorption.  
**Reference:** General music-and-attention literature; user testing preferred ~90 BPM over higher tempos.

## Experimental / Plausible Mechanisms

### Mesolimbic Reward Engagement
Using pleasant, toe-tapping grooves and positive valence to engage the ventral tegmental area (VTA) → nucleus accumbens pathway, potentially tagging the material with a mild reward signal.  
**Why included:** Music is known to activate reward circuitry; the hope is that this makes the learning material more motivating and memorable.  
**Status:** Plausible but not directly proven for educational content.  
**Reference:** Salimpoor et al. on music and dopamine; general reward-learning literature.

### Emotional Tagging via Amygdala
Positive, inviting musical mood may help the amygdala tag the material as emotionally relevant.  
**Why included:** Emotionally salient events are often better remembered.  
**Status:** Supported for strong emotion; milder positive valence is less certain.  
**Reference:** McGaugh on emotional modulation of memory.

### Voice Distinction (Male/Female or Single Consistent Voice)
Experimenting with single clear voice vs. male/female dialogue for question-answer pairs.  
**Why included:** Distinct voices can improve segmentation and source memory; consistent voice can improve cohesion and earworm strength.  
**Status:** Mixed; user testing preferred single voice for core mappings.  
**Reference:** General auditory scene analysis and source-memory literature.

### Low-Clutter Visual Backgrounds + Large Subtitles
Pleasant but simple scenes with large high-contrast text overlays.  
**Why included:** Supports dual coding while minimizing extraneous cognitive load.  
**Status:** Aligns with Mayer’s coherence and signaling principles.  
**Reference:** Mayer’s multimedia principles.

### Binaural Beats (tested, not adopted)
Short experiments with binaural beats for focus.  
**Why included:** Meta-analyses show small-to-moderate average effects on attention, but results are inconsistent and entrainment claims are weak.  
**Status:** Not used in the core method after review.  
**Reference:** Garcia-Argibay et al. (2019); Basu & Banerjee (2022/2023).

## Practical Testing Notes
- ~90 BPM and clear single-voice delivery produced the strongest subjective “sticky” and pleasant-to-loop results.
- Male/female dialogue was interesting but less cohesive for heavy repetition.
- Heavy reverb, vocoder, or free-rhythm experimental styles reduced lyric intelligibility and were adapted or set aside.
- Dual coding (subtitles + audio) was consistently high-value.
